I read on screens for about ten years, more or less exclusively. It was practical. The books were cheaper, they arrived instantly, and a whole shelf fit in a coat pocket. I did not think I had lost anything. Then a friend lent me a paperback with her notes in the margins, and I read it in two evenings, and I could not remember the last time a book had done that.

So I tried a year on paper. Not as an experiment, exactly, more as a way to check.

## What I expected

I expected to read less, because paper is slower to get hold of. I expected to miss the dictionary. I expected the sentimental things people say about the smell of books to remain sentimental.

All three happened, and none of them mattered as much as I thought.

## What actually happened

The difference was not speed or convenience. It was memory. I can tell you where on the page a sentence sat in most of the books I read this year, and roughly how far into the book, because the book had a thickness on either side of my thumb. On a screen every page was the same page. I finished books and could not have told you their shape.

A few other things, in no particular order:

- I reread more. Flipping back is a gesture, not a search.
- I stopped at the ends of chapters instead of at the ends of battery.
- I bought fewer books, and I finished more of them.
- Marginalia came back. A pencil in a book is a conversation; a highlight in an app is a bookmark.[^1]
- I read in the bath again, with the calm that comes from knowing the worst case is a warped paperback.

## The obvious caveat

None of this is an argument against screens. Someone once said to me, about a different medium entirely:

> The best format is the one that gets read.

That is still true. Half the world reads on a phone because a phone is what they have, and I am not going to tell anyone that a paperback would make them a better person. I am only reporting what changed for me, which is that the books started staying with me again.

## Where I landed

I still read on a screen: on trains, in the dark, when a book is out of print. But the default has flipped, and the thing I notice most is that I no longer feel behind. There is a stack on the table, it is about six books deep, and it is not going anywhere. That turns out to be the whole point.

[^1]: The best used bookshops know this and price a book with good marginalia higher, not lower. A stranger's argument in the margin is a second book for free.
