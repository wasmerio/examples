Editing other people's work is easy in one specific way: I do not remember what they meant. I only have what is on the page. When I read my own draft, the meaning arrives before the sentence does, and I skip straight past whatever is broken. Every trick I have for self-editing is really a trick for forgetting.

## Put distance between you and the draft

The oldest advice is still the best: wait. A night is good, a week is better. When I cannot wait, I change the text until it looks unfamiliar:

1. Print it, or at least read it in a different application from the one it was written in.
2. Change the typeface to something I dislike.
3. Read it aloud, slowly, and mark every place where I stumble or take a breath the punctuation did not ask for.
4. Read the paragraphs in reverse order, last to first, so the argument cannot carry me.

Number three catches the most. The ear is a better editor than the eye, because the ear cannot skim.

## Cut before you polish

The temptation is to fix the sentence in front of you. Resist it until you have decided whether the sentence should exist. Most of my drafts lose a fifth of their length in the first pass, and almost all of that is the second paragraph, where I was still warming up, and the last one, where I was saying it again in case you missed it.

Pascal apologised for a long letter by saying he had not had time to write a short one.[^1] The line is quoted so often because it is true, and because cutting is real work that shows nowhere. Nobody sees the paragraph you removed. They only feel the pace.

## A short checklist

When the structure is settled, I go through the piece one more time with a list I have refined over many years. The current version is short:

| Look for | Usually means |
| --- | --- |
| Two adjectives in a row | Neither one is right |
| "Very", "really", "quite" | The next word is too weak |
| A sentence that begins with "There is" | The subject is hiding |
| Three sentences of the same length | Read it aloud again |
| The word "just" | Delete it and see |

None of this is a rule. The list exists so that I have something mechanical to do at the end, when my judgement is tired and my attachment to the draft is strongest.

> Your job as an editor is to be the first reader who does not love the writer.

An editor I trained under said that to me in my first month, and I have never found a better description of the job, including when the writer is me.

[^1]: The letter is the sixteenth of the *Provincial Letters*, from 1656. The original French says he made it longer only because he lacked the leisure to make it shorter. The translations vary, the point does not.
